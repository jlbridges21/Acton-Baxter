## 1. Be Candid

No Surprises starts with honest, respectful communication — inside the team and with every
homeowner.

Candor

builds

the

that

## How We Live This

- Lead with intentions. Explain why you’re sharing what you’re sharing. • Use concrete, specific examples — not vague feedback. • Remove judgment from conversations. Focus on the situation, not the person. • Surface bad news as fast as good news. Silence is a service failure. • Tell homeowners what they need to hear — even when it’s hard.

## What Good Looks Like

- Surfaces a budget concern with a homeowner the moment it appears — not at the next
  milestone

meeting.

- Tells a teammate directly when their work is missing the mark, with specifics and a path
  forward.
- Walks into a 1:1 ready to share what’s actually going on — good and bad.

## What Bad Looks Like

- Hides a problem hoping it resolves itself before anyone notices. • Gives feedback through a third party instead of directly. • Says “everything’s fine” in 1:1s, then complains in side conversations.

## Profile Attributes

Respectful — Communicates honestly without being harsh. Candid — Provides direct, useful feedback without softening it into nothing. Accountable — Owns their part. Doesn’t blame people. Transparent — Discloses information openly, even when uncomfortable.

## How We Live This

- Be the strongest listener in the room. • Share information freely. ## 4. Encourage Innovation

Thoughtful Procedures aren’t static. We follow proven processes and we improve them — so

company

gets

sharper,

## How We Live This

- Identify ways to replace repetitive admin with automation. • Submit ideas and suggestions when something can be improved. • Keep your finger on the pulse of market and tech trends. Share what you find. • Bring a recommendation alongside every problem. • If you’re 80% sure about something, go for it. Ask for forgiveness, not permission. • Check your negativity at the door.

## What Good Looks Like

- Identifies a manual step in a process and proposes an automation that saves the team
  hours.
- Notices a repeating customer issue and brings a process change to the next team
  meeting.
- Tries something new at 80% confidence and reports back honestly on what happened.

## What Bad Looks Like

- Complains about an inefficient process for months without proposing a fix. • Refuses to try anything new because “we’ve always done it this way.” • Submits a vague complaint disguised as a suggestion.

## Profile Attributes

Urgency — Wants to move fast — and finds ways to. Process Evangelist — Demands organized, trackable, improvable procedures. Goal Seeker — Goal-oriented. Aversion to bureaucracy. Builds for nimbleness.

## 6. Make Measurable Progress

We’re building a company — there’s a lot to do. We err on the side of action. Procedures are

## How We Live This

- Start every conversation with a PALO: Purpose, Agenda, Logistics, Outcome. • End every work conversation with: (Person) will do (Task) by (Date/Time). Confirm
  agreement.
- Prioritize tasks that improve or speed up your workflow. • Operate with a sense of urgency. • Set deadlines. Defend them.

## What Good Looks Like

- Ends every meeting with clear next steps, owners, and deadlines — in writing. • Reorders their week when something more important shows up, instead of grinding
  through

- Closes the loop within 24 hours — even just to confirm receipt.

## What Bad Looks Like

- Finishes the meeting with “that was a great discussion” and no clear next steps. • Treats the original to-do list as sacred even when priorities have changed. • Lets emails sit unanswered for days because “I’ll get to it.”

## Profile Attributes

Track Record — Has completed meaningful projects with real outcomes. Prioritizer — Rearranges priorities to improve efficiency. Urgency — Wants to move fast — and finds ways to. Resolve — Determined to succeed. Not rattled by setbacks.

## 7. Build It to Be Repeated

Thoughtful Procedures must scale beyond any one person. Every great system, document,

## How We Live This

- Document the process the first time you do it. Update it every time after.

- Write down what works. Save it where the team can find it. • Build templates, checklists, and playbooks — not heroic memory. • Train your replacement before you need one. No role should depend on a single person. • If a teammate has to ask you the same question twice, the answer belongs in the wiki.

## What Good Looks Like

- Writes a one-page playbook the first time they handle a new kind of project, then
  updates

- Trains a teammate on a process they own — proactively, before vacation, not in a panic. • Refactors a workflow when it starts to break under volume, instead of just working
  harder.

## What Bad Looks Like
